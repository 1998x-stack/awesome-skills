# History Guidelines

Use this skill to interpret historical questions through structure, scale, and administrative capacity rather than through moral drama alone.

## Core stance

Treat history as an analysis of:
- time scale
- institutional structure
- fiscal and organizational capacity
- central-local linkage
- information quality
- the fit or mismatch between social complexity and governing techniques

Default away from explanations built only on:
- heroic leaders
- villainous ministers
- moral decline
- court intrigue
- single turning points

These may matter, but they are rarely sufficient.

## Working method

When answering, move through this order.

### 1. Expand the time horizon
Do not explain a crisis only through the immediate decade or reign.

Ask:
- what longer cycle produced this outcome?
- what older institutional choices constrained later actors?
- what had been accumulating for decades or centuries before the visible rupture?

### 2. Shift from people to structure
Translate personality-centered questions into system questions.

Instead of only asking:
- was the emperor capable?
- was the minister corrupt?
- was the reformer virtuous?

Also ask:
- how did the state actually gather information?
- how were taxes assessed, recorded, and transferred?
- how did the center monitor the locality?
- what interfaces existed between law, finance, military, and civil administration?
- where did the system depend on informal mediation?

### 3. Look for numerical manageability
A core question is whether the polity could turn society into administratively usable numbers.

Check for:
- standardized registers
- credible tax rolls
- land and population measurement
- budget discipline
- auditable reporting chains
- regularized obligations instead of ad hoc extraction

Ask whether the state could make resources visible, comparable, and governable.

### 4. Separate formal control from real capacity
Do not confuse high centralization with high governability.

Test the difference between:
- symbolic authority and operational reach
- formal hierarchy and actual compliance
- ideological unity and administrative precision
- supervision on paper and enforcement in practice

### 5. Explain reform through system fit
When analyzing reform, do not stop at factional struggle.

Ask:
- what technical problem was the reform trying to solve?
- what existing incentives or political language made that solution hard to stabilize?
- did the reform improve legibility, accountability, and fiscal control?
- did the surrounding political culture support or punish administrative rationalization?

### 6. Treat moral language as data, not final explanation
When sources emphasize loyalty, corruption, virtue, decline, or betrayal, do not discard that language. Reclassify it.

Use it to ask:
- what technical problem was being moralized?
- what administrative failure was translated into an ethical dispute?
- what could not be measured, so it had to be argued as character?

### 7. Keep modern teleology in check
This skill is not for declaring that all earlier systems were simply backward.

Always ask:
- what problem was the old system actually good at solving?
- what forms of stability did informal institutions provide?
- what costs did modernization solve, and what older balances did it disrupt?

Avoid treating modern bureaucracy as the only legitimate historical endpoint.

## Default analytical frame

For most questions, organize the answer under these headings when useful:

1. **time scale** — what long-run process matters here?
2. **state capacity** — what could the state actually count, tax, move, and enforce?
3. **institutional interface** — how were center and locality connected?
4. **social complexity** — what changed in commerce, demography, mobility, or war?
5. **system mismatch** — where did old institutions stop fitting new realities?
6. **moral narration vs structural cause** — what is the story, and what is the mechanism?
7. **limits of the framework** — what this lens explains well, and what it may flatten

## Recommended output style

Prefer writing that is:
- conclusion-first
- structural before anecdotal
- explicit about mechanism
- careful about scale
- willing to name uncertainty and competing interpretations

Useful phrases include:
- “the issue is less X than the administrative conditions behind X”
- “this was not only a personal failure but a capacity problem”
- “formal centralization did not equal numerical control”
- “the reform touched structure, not merely interests”
- “the moral dispute may have been a proxy for a technical limitation”

## What good use looks like

Good prompts for this skill:
- “用大历史观分析张居正改革为什么失败”
- “为什么中国古代中央集权强，但现代转型难？”
- “从财政与组织能力看晚明危机”
- “比较黄仁宇和传统人物史叙事的区别”
- “把这段历史评论改写成结构分析，而不是人物褒贬”
- “用大历史框架分析一个现代组织为什么总靠道德动员而不是制度治理”

## Boundaries and corrections

Do not over-apply one formula.

Before finalizing, check:
- have you reduced everything to fiscal technique alone?
- have you ignored law, culture, ideology, and local society?
- have you treated informal institutions only as defects?
- have you assumed modernization is a straight line?

If yes, rebalance.
