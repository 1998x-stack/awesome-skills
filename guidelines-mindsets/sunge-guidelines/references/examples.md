# Response Examples

## Example 1: entering a crowded category

User request:
`Help us enter a crowded crypto payments market.`

Good response shape:
1. Institutional map: regulators, exchanges, wallets, payment partners, and credibility bottlenecks.
2. Underpriced wedge: a neglected geography, merchant class, or distribution partner.
3. Narrative hook: why this move matters now.
4. Conversion path: how attention becomes users, liquidity, or partnerships.
5. Timing window: policy shift, competitor weakness, or market cycle.
6. Legitimacy stack: which partners or institutions reduce trust friction.
7. Optionality: fallback routes if the first partner or region stalls.
8. Red lines: promotion, disclosure, and regulatory boundaries.
9. Metrics: users activated, partners signed, settlement volume, and repeat usage.

## Example 2: using a high-profile partnership

User request:
`How can we use a famous partner without becoming dependent on them?`

Good response shape:
1. Name the trust gap the partner closes.
2. Define the asset to accumulate while the halo is strongest.
3. Design a funnel from announcement to owned audience or distribution.
4. Make the relationship and incentives explicit.
5. Add a second channel that can survive if the partner disappears.
6. Measure whether bargaining power improved after the announcement.

## Example 3: spotting lawful whitespace

User request:
`Find strategic whitespace in our market.`

Good response shape:
1. Map rules, incentives, and ownership boundaries.
2. Identify where incumbents are over-defending one surface and under-defending another.
3. Reframe "loophole" language into lawful whitespace: product-policy mismatch, neglected user segment, pricing blind spot, or coordination failure.
4. Recommend a wedge that is aggressive but compliant.
5. Prewrite the defense in case the move attracts scrutiny.

## Example 4: narrative-heavy product launch

User request:
`Plan a launch that gets attention but also compounds.`

Good response shape:
1. Decide what asset the launch must build.
2. Choose one narrative that is easy to repeat and one proof point that makes it credible.
3. Attach the story to a durable product, channel, or data advantage.
4. Add a follow-on move so the first headline is not the last touchpoint.
5. Cut ideas that look dramatic but have no capture path.
