# Jenova-inspired design principles

Use this file when the request explicitly references Jenova Chen, Journey, Flower, Sky, emotional design, humane multiplayer, or “experience-first” design.

## 1. Start from the intended human feeling
Do not begin with content quantity or feature count. Begin with the feeling, relationship, or human meaning the experience should create. Then derive mechanics, pacing, art direction, audio behavior, and social rules from that target.

Useful prompts:
- what emotion should the player remember after they stop playing?
- what part of life or human connection does this idea echo?
- which mechanics are only present because other games have them?

## 2. Separate content from system
A game contains both content and system.
- **content**: the specific experience, fantasy, symbolism, and meaning being conveyed
- **system**: the rules, controls, pacing, feedback, and interactions that deliver that content

A concept can have strong content but weak system, or strong system but shallow content. Diagnose both separately.

## 3. Design for flow through player agency
Do not rely only on passive difficulty tuning. Prefer embedded choices that let players regulate pace, risk, and challenge through play.

Look for:
- optional danger
- self-selected tempo
- recoverable failure
- multiple valid play intensities
- low-friction mastery ramps

## 4. Shape an emotional arc
Memorable experiences are paced. Define how the player should feel at the start, midpoint, crisis, climax, and ending. Use systems, level transitions, music, and visual scale to support those beats.

Common failure modes:
- flat intensity
- nonstop escalation with no contrast
- emotional mismatch between mechanics and presentation
- strong ending not earned by prior play

## 5. Reduce interface noise when designing intimacy
If the goal is companionship, awe, vulnerability, or trust, reduce systems that force social performance or status comparison unless they are essential.

Be cautious with:
- public ranking pressure
- loud identity signals that increase judgment
- mandatory voice or text chat
- blame-heavy role dependence
- punitive failure that turns allies into liabilities

## 6. Let kindness happen through play
Prosocial games do not only add friendly chat features. They create small, low-cost, high-meaning actions that let players help, wait, protect, guide, recharge, or reassure one another through mechanics.

## 7. Design for adulthood and relevance
Pressure-test whether the design has emotional or intellectual relevance beyond childish domination fantasies. The question is not whether the game is “serious,” but whether it touches something recognizably human.

## 8. Accessibility is part of emotional reach
If the intended experience is meant for broad audiences, remove avoidable mechanical, cognitive, and social barriers. Simplicity is not the opposite of depth.

## 9. Trust is a retention system
For social products, meaningful relationships, safety, and mutual care can be as important as power progression. Long-term retention may come from belonging, ritual, and shared memory rather than constant extrinsic rewards.

## 10. Protect the core feeling from product pressures
When working on f2p or live-service products, identify where monetization, streaks, friction, competitive scarcity, or spammy events would weaken the intended emotional promise.
